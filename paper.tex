\begin{abstract}
Remaining useful life (RUL) prediction is central to prognostics and health management in industrial IoT systems, where sensor streams from rotating machinery combine slow degradation trends with sharper, nonlinear fault dynamics. One way to help a sequence model separate these two components is to fit a classical time-series model to each sensor channel and give the network both the raw signal and the fitted trend, an idea with some support in prior work on other kinds of equipment. We test it on NASA's C-MAPSS turbofan benchmark, and we test it twice: once with the fixed ARIMA(1,1,0) order common in prior work, and once with a per-channel, AIC-selected order, comparing both hybrid variants against a plain LSTM and a Random Forest baseline on all four C-MAPSS sub-datasets, each run under six random seeds. Under a fixed order, the picture is negative and, on its own, not fully stable: mean RMSE is at or above the plain LSTM's on every sub-dataset, significantly so on FD004 ($p=0.004$), and an earlier three-seed version of the same comparison had instead flagged FD001, not FD004, as significant, a reversal we report directly. Under a properly selected order, the result changes: the selected-order hybrid significantly and consistently outperforms the fixed-order hybrid on two of four sub-datasets (FD002, FD004; $p=0.031$, 6 of 6 seeds each), and on FD002 it also significantly outperforms the plain LSTM baseline itself (13.77 vs.\ 14.59 cycles RMSE, $p=0.031$, 6 of 6 seeds), the one result we consider a genuine, seed-robust win for the trend-augmented architecture. A second, unrelated baseline, a CNN-LSTM with no ARIMA feature, shows the same dataset-dependent pattern: significantly worse on FD002 ($p<0.001$) while strongest in the table, though not quite significantly, on FD003 ($p=0.091$). The honest summary is not that trend augmentation fails, but that it fails under the field's default fixed-order convention and can succeed once that convention is checked rather than assumed, a distinction we could only draw by insisting on six-seed replication for every claim, including the ones that first looked negative.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Unplanned downtime in industrial and manufacturing settings carries substantial operational and safety cost, motivating a large body of work on prognostics and health management (PHM), the discipline of estimating a system's remaining useful life (RUL) from streaming sensor data so that maintenance can be scheduled proactively rather than reactively \cite{saxena2008}. Industrial IoT deployments now produce continuous, high-volume, multivariate sensor telemetry (vibration, temperature, pressure, rotational speed) from equipment such as turbines, pumps, and CNC machinery, a big-data setting at the deployment scale even though the benchmark used in this paper, a few hundred simulated engines per sub-dataset, is not itself large by that standard. A recent survey by Khan et al.\ \cite{survey2025} catalogs the resulting breadth of deep-learning-based PdM methods (CNNs, autoencoders, GANs, transformers, foundation models) across supervised, transfer, and federated learning paradigms, giving some sense of how active, and how fragmented, this area has become.

Two modeling traditions address RUL estimation differently. ARIMA captures the smooth trend and autocorrelation structure of a single degrading signal well, but assumes linearity and handles multivariate interaction poorly. LSTM networks \cite{lstm1997} learn nonlinear temporal dependencies directly from raw multivariate windows and are a standard RUL approach, but a randomly initialized LSTM must learn both the trend and the nonlinear fault dynamics from data alone, inefficient for short histories or noisy multi-condition operation. It is natural to give the LSTM a head start on the trend part.

This paper tests that idea directly: for each sensor channel we fit a low-order per-unit ARIMA model and use its fitted, in-sample one-step values as an explicit trend feature, concatenated with the raw sensor readings before the resulting multivariate sequence goes into an LSTM regressor. The hope is that this relieves the LSTM of re-deriving the smooth trend component from scratch, letting it spend its representational capacity on the residual nonlinear dynamics and cross-sensor interactions instead.

We evaluate this across all four C-MAPSS sub-datasets: single condition, single fault mode (FD001); single condition, two fault modes (FD003); multi-condition, single fault mode (FD002); and multi-condition, two fault modes (FD004). More importantly, after an early result made us suspicious of our own conclusions, we do not stop at a single training run or a single small batch of them. We repeat every comparison across six random seeds, and separately check whether a fixed ARIMA order was itself the problem via a per-channel order-selection variant, reporting what changed each time we added more evidence, including cases where it overturned a finding we had already started to explain.

Our contributions are: (1) a lightweight, per-sensor ARIMA trend-augmentation scheme for LSTM-based RUL regression requiring no additional labels; (2) an evaluation across all four C-MAPSS sub-datasets, with regime-aware normalization \cite{regimenorm2018} for the multi-condition subsets, against plain-LSTM, CNN-LSTM, and Random Forest baselines, each averaged over six seeds; (3) a robustness check using per-channel ARIMA order selection and a gated-fusion mechanism in place of a fixed order and naive concatenation, both just as seed-sensitive as the main comparison; (4) a seed-averaged window-length ablation; and (5) a documented account of how this paper's headline finding changed as we added more seeds, illustrating why single- and small-multi-seed comparisons deserve skepticism.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{RUL estimation on C-MAPSS.} The NASA C-MAPSS turbofan dataset \cite{saxena2008} is the standard benchmark for data-driven RUL estimation. Ramasso and Saxena \cite{cmapssbenchmark2014} laid out early benchmarking protocols across similarity-based and regression approaches. Recent work moves toward deep sequence and hybrid architectures: Muthukumar and Philip \cite{cnnlstm2024} combine convolutional feature extraction with LSTM temporal modeling, Chowdhury et al.\ \cite{biclstm2026} propose a residual-corrected bidirectional LSTM separating a coarse trend prediction from a learned residual correction, related to but architecturally distinct from the ARIMA-based decomposition used here, and Sharma \cite{sharma2025} reports an attention-based bidirectional LSTM on all four sub-datasets. We do not compare our numbers against Sharma's directly, since the two setups may not share the same RUL cap, window length, or train/validation protocol, and an uncontrolled cross-paper RMSE comparison would not be a defensible claim either way.

\textbf{Multi-condition RUL estimation.} FD002 and FD004 introduce six discrete operating regimes and, in FD004's case, two concurrent fault modes, both increasing estimation difficulty relative to FD001's single-condition setting. Su and Fang \cite{multiheadattn2026} propose a multi-head attention fusion network that decomposes degradation into trend, operational-state embedding, and residual-noise components, a philosophy that motivates our own trend/residual split, though implemented with ARIMA rather than a learned embedding.

\textbf{Hybrid ARIMA-deep learning models.} Outside turbofan RUL, ARIMA-LSTM hybrids have been applied to remaining-useful-life estimation of water-hydraulic on/off valves \cite{arimalstmvalve2022}, where ARIMA models the linear degradation trend and an auxiliary classifier detects health-state transitions, and to battery life estimation \cite{arimacnnlstm2024}, where ARIMA-smoothed inputs feed an attention-based CNN-LSTM. Both report the hybrid approach helping; neither states how many seeds its numbers rest on. Given our own headline finding changed twice as we added seeds (Sections V, VI), we raise this as an open question for the hybrid-forecasting literature rather than a claim about either paper.

\textbf{Reproducibility and seed sensitivity in machine learning.} The instability we document in Section V is a specific instance of a broader problem other subfields have already named. Henderson et al.\ \cite{henderson2018} showed headline deep reinforcement learning results routinely fail to replicate across random seeds. Lipton and Steinhardt \cite{lipton2018} catalog a related failure, distinguishing which part of an empirical gain a change is actually responsible for, close to the mistake our own three-seed FD001 result nearly encoded into this paper. Bouthillier et al.\ \cite{bouthillier2019} make the point most relevant to us: a result can be exactly reproducible while still not robust to small changes in setup, the relationship between our fixed-seed and multi-seed FD001 results. To our knowledge this caution has not previously been raised inside the C-MAPSS RUL literature. A recent example: Goel et al.\ \cite{goel2026}, on this exact benchmark within weeks of our own experiments, state plainly that ``all experiments use random seed 42 for reproducibility,'' illustrating the conflation Bouthillier et al.\ warn about. We see our contribution as importing an already-known concern into a subfield that has not yet widely adopted it.

Our approach differs from prior turbofan RUL work in using ARIMA purely as a per-channel feature-engineering step, an explicit trend covariate per sensor, rather than a separate forecaster fused post-hoc with a deep model's prediction, and in evaluating it across all four regimes, seeds, and two order-selection strategies rather than a single convenient run.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Datasets}
We use all four NASA C-MAPSS sub-datasets \cite{saxena2008}: \textbf{FD001} (100 train / 100 test engines, 1 condition, 1 fault mode: HPC degradation), \textbf{FD002} (260 train / 259 test, 6 conditions, 1 fault mode), \textbf{FD003} (100 train / 100 test, 1 condition, 2 fault modes: HPC and fan degradation), and \textbf{FD004} (249 train / 248 test, 6 conditions, 2 fault modes). Each unit reports 3 operational settings and 21 sensor channels per cycle; training units run to failure while test units are truncated at an arbitrary cycle, and the task is to predict the true remaining cycles at that truncation point.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Following standard practice \cite{cmapssbenchmark2014}, we cap the training RUL label at 125 cycles, reflecting the empirical observation that engine degradation is roughly constant before this point and roughly linear afterward. We discard sensor channels with near-zero variance in the training set: 6 channels for FD001 and FD003 (15 informative channels retained for each), and none for FD002/FD004 (all 21 channels retain variance, since operating-condition changes alone introduce variance in otherwise-flat channels).

\textbf{Regime-aware normalization.} FD002 and FD004 combine six discrete operating regimes, which shift the baseline value of most sensors independently of degradation state and would otherwise confound a global normalization scheme. Following Li, Ding, and Sun \cite{regimenorm2018}, we cluster the three operational-setting dimensions into $K$ regimes with $K$-means on the training set ($K{=}6$ for FD002/FD004; $K{=}1$ for FD001/FD003, the same code path reducing to ordinary global normalization), assign each test row to its nearest regime centroid, and $z$-score normalize each sensor using regime-specific training statistics, clipping to $\pm 6$ std to control outliers in sparsely populated regimes.

\subsection{ARIMA Trend-Feature Extraction}
For each engine unit and each retained sensor channel, we independently fit an ARIMA model to that channel's cycle-indexed, regime-normalized time series. Writing $y_t$ for a given channel's reading at cycle $t$, an ARIMA(1,1,0) model, our main-comparison default, treats the first difference $\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$ as an AR(1) process,
\begin{equation}
\Delta y_t = \phi \, \Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
\end{equation}
equivalently $y_t = y_{t-1} + \phi (y_{t-1} - y_{t-2}) + \varepsilon_t$. We fit $\phi$ and $\sigma^2$ once per (unit, sensor) pair by maximum likelihood over that unit's entire available series (the full run-to-failure history for a training unit, or the full truncated history up to the prediction cycle for a test unit), then take the in-sample one-step-ahead fitted value $\hat{y}_t$, the Kalman-filter prediction of $y_t$ from $y_1,\ldots,y_{t-1}$ under the fitted parameters, as the trend feature at each cycle. The fitted value at cycle $t$ therefore uses no reading at or after $t$, but the parameters $\phi,\sigma^2$ themselves are estimated from the unit's whole available series rather than an expanding or rolling window; Section VII discusses the resulting train/test asymmetry, since training units are complete run-to-failure histories and test units are truncated. Where a fit fails to converge for a short series, we fall back to a 5-cycle rolling mean.

\textbf{Order selection variant.} Section V-C reports a second version where, instead of fixing the order at $(1,1,0)$, we fit four candidates per (unit, sensor) pair, $(1,1,0)$, $(0,1,1)$, $(1,1,1)$, $(2,1,0)$, and keep the fitted values with the lowest AIC. Windowing, architecture, and training are identical between variants; only the trend feature's construction differs.

\subsection{Hybrid Model Architecture}
We construct sliding windows of length $W$ cycles ($W{=}30$ in our main experiments; see Section V-D for a window-length ablation) over the concatenation of raw sensor features and their ARIMA trend features. Each window is passed through a 2-layer LSTM (hidden size 64) whose cell updates follow the standard formulation \cite{lstm1997}
\begin{align}
f_t &= \sigma(W_f [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f), \quad i_t = \sigma(W_i [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i) \nonumber\\
\tilde{c}_t &= \tanh(W_c [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c), \quad o_t = \sigma(W_o [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o) \nonumber\\
c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t)
\end{align}
followed by a feed-forward regression head ($64 \to 32 \to 1$) predicting capped RUL from the final hidden state $h_W$. Training minimizes mean squared error,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(\mathrm{RUL}_i - \widehat{\mathrm{RUL}}_i\right)^2,
\end{equation}
with Adam, using an engine-level held-out validation split (15\% of training units, never seen during training) for early stopping.

\subsection{Baselines}
We compare against (i) a \emph{Random Forest} regressor (300 trees, max depth 10), a non-sequential baseline trained on per-cycle flat feature snapshots from every training cycle rather than just each engine's terminal cycle, since terminal-cycle-only training would teach a degenerate near-zero-RUL target given that training engines run to failure; (ii) a \emph{plain LSTM}, identical architecture and training to the hybrid model but using only raw sensor features, isolating the trend feature's effect; and (iii) a \emph{CNN-LSTM}, a two-layer 1D convolutional feature extractor (32 then 64 channels, kernel size 3) feeding the same two-layer LSTM, representative of the CNN-LSTM family common in recent C-MAPSS RUL literature \cite{cnnlstm2024}, using no ARIMA feature but the identical protocol, seed set, and early-stopping criterion, so its Table~\ref{tab:main_results} results are directly comparable.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
Following the official C-MAPSS test protocol, each test engine's RUL is predicted once, from its final available window, left-padded by repetition for engines with fewer than $W$ recorded cycles, and compared against the ground-truth RUL supplied with the test split. We report RMSE, MAE, and the asymmetric PHM scoring function of \cite{saxena2008}: writing $d_i = \widehat{\mathrm{RUL}}_i - \mathrm{RUL}_i$,
\begin{equation}
s_i =
\begin{cases}
\exp(-d_i/13) - 1, & d_i < 0 \ \text{(early/conservative)} \\
\exp(d_i/10) - 1, & d_i \geq 0 \ \text{(late/over-optimistic)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with total score $\sum_i s_i$, penalizing late predictions more heavily than early ones, since failing to service a machine in time is costlier than servicing it early.

\textbf{What changed between three seeds and six.} We want to walk through this in some detail because we think it is the most useful thing in the paper. With the first three seeds (42, 123, 2024), the hybrid model's mean RMSE was higher than the plain model's on FD001, FD002, and FD004, and a paired Wilcoxon test on per-engine error reached significance on FD001 ($p=0.007$) and nowhere else. Adding three more seeds (7, 99, 314) changed the specific answer. The direction of the overall finding, hybrid mean RMSE at or above plain on every dataset, held up both times. Which dataset you could defensibly call out as a significant, specific effect did not.

\textbf{A second, architecturally different baseline.} Both LSTM variants share the same recurrent backbone, so a null result for the trend feature says nothing about a differently structured model. We add a CNN-LSTM baseline (Section III-E) under the identical protocol (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). The result does not point one way: on FD003, CNN-LSTM is best-in-table on all three metrics (RMSE 13.90 vs.\ 14.72 plain), just short of significance ($p=0.091$); on FD002, the opposite holds, significantly worse ($p<0.001$), the same dataset where order selection produced this paper's clearest win. FD001 and FD004 sit within noise ($p=0.842$, $p=0.429$).

\subsection{ARIMA Order Selection}
The main comparison fixes the ARIMA order at $(1,1,0)$ for every sensor and every dataset. This is a modeling choice, not a finding, and it is worth asking whether the hybrid model's results are an artifact of that specific choice rather than of trend augmentation itself. We re-ran the ARIMA feature extraction with per-channel AIC-based order selection among four candidates (Section III-C) and re-trained both LSTM variants on the resulting features, at all six seeds across all four datasets, the same replication we used for the main comparison, after an early two-seed version of this check (reported in an earlier draft of this paper) suggested the answer might itself be seed-dependent and was too thin to trust on its own.

Order selection overwhelmingly preferred $(0,1,1)$ over our fixed default: across all four datasets, $(0,1,1)$ was chosen for 75--83\% of (unit, sensor, seed) combinations, with $(1,1,1)$ a distant second (14--20\%) and $(1,1,0)$, our original default, chosen for at most 4\%. Table~\ref{tab:orderselect} reports the resulting hybrid-model RMSE, mean $\pm$ std over six seeds, alongside the fixed-order hybrid and the plain LSTM.

Unlike the two-seed version we started with, this is not a wash. A paired Wilcoxon test, selected order vs.\ fixed order, reaches significance on FD002 and FD004 ($p=0.031$ on both, the smallest attainable $p$-value at $n=6$ paired samples, with the selected order better on 6 of 6 seeds each time) and does not on FD001 ($p=0.688$, better on 4 of 6) or FD003 ($p=0.219$, better on 5 of 6). The more direct question, whether the order-selected hybrid beats the \emph{plain} LSTM rather than just the fixed-order hybrid, gives a sharper answer still: on FD002, it does, significantly ($p=0.031$, 6 of 6 seeds, 13.77 vs.\ 14.59), which is the one place in this paper where the hybrid architecture beats the plain baseline with the same statistical confidence we used to declare FD004's fixed-order disadvantage significant in Section V-B. On FD004, order selection erases the fixed-order disadvantage but does not turn it into an advantage (14.90 vs.\ 14.92 plain, $p=1.000$, essentially a tie). FD001 and FD003 stay ties either way.

Read together with Section V-B, the honest summary is not ``the hybrid model does not help'' but ``the hybrid model does not help \emph{under a naively fixed ARIMA order}, and on at least one dataset, FD002, a properly selected order turns that into a real, seed-robust improvement.'' We did not expect this when we started the order-selection check; our working assumption going in, informed by the two-seed version, was that it would turn out to be more noise. Six seeds said otherwise on two of four datasets.

Order selection's benefit comes at a compute cost. Fitting all four candidate orders instead of one produces a single-CPU wall-clock overhead of 2.04$\times$ (FD001), 3.11$\times$ (FD002, largest where the benefit is also largest), 1.68$\times$ (FD003), and 2.93$\times$ (FD004), a one-time preprocessing cost, not a per-inference one. Concatenating trend features also roughly doubles LSTM input width, raising parameter count by 7.2\% on the 15-sensor datasets and 9.8\% on the 21-sensor ones. Weigh a roughly 3$\times$ preprocessing cost and a sub-10\% parameter increase against a gain real on two of four datasets (Table~\ref{tab:orderselect}) and absent on the other two.

\subsection{Window-Length Ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} report a window-length ablation on FD001 ($W \in \{10, 20, 30, 50\}$), each trained with a shorter early-stopping patience than the main experiments for faster iteration across four window sizes. We initially ran this at three seeds (42, 123, 2024) and, consistent with the seed-sensitivity we found in Section V-A, extended it to the same six seeds used everywhere else in the paper before treating the pattern as reliable. The three additional seeds changed the picture. At three seeds, the hybrid model's advantage appeared to grow monotonically with window length up to $W{=}30$ and then reverse at $W{=}50$. At six seeds, that monotonic story does not hold: $W{=}20$ and $W{=}30$ both show a real, consistent hybrid advantage (0.68 and 0.54 cycles), while $W{=}10$ and $W{=}50$ are both close to flat, with the sign of the small difference at each going in opposite directions across the two short-and-long extremes ($-0.02$ at $W{=}10$, $-0.17$ at $W{=}50$) rather than showing a clean reversal. The honest summary is a middle-window advantage bounded on both sides by noise, not a smooth peak-and-reverse curve.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{The headline finding is about evaluation, and it points to a real fix.} We went into this project expecting a yes-or-no answer to whether a trend feature helps an LSTM predict RUL. Under a fixed ARIMA order, the specific answer moved as we checked our own work more carefully: at three seeds, FD001 looked like a clean, significant, explainable disadvantage for the hybrid model; at six seeds, that result was gone and FD004 had taken its place, a domain-specific instance of the pattern Henderson et al.\ \cite{henderson2018} and Bouthillier et al.\ \cite{bouthillier2019} document in reinforcement learning: a configuration can be exactly reproducible and still not representative of what the method does on average. We do not think the story ends there, though. Once we powered the order-selection check to the same six seeds, it stopped looking like a third source of noise and started looking like an explanation: on FD002, replacing the fixed order with a properly AIC-selected one turns a nominal disadvantage into a significant, 6-of-6-seed advantage over the plain LSTM (Section V-C). On FD004, it erases the fixed-order disadvantage entirely. On FD001 and FD003, it does neither.

\textbf{What we think is actually going on.} ARIMA is fit to each unit's full cycle-indexed series (Section III-C), not to the 30-cycle window the LSTM consumes; the trend feature at each cycle within a window comes from that unit-level fit. An ARIMA(1,1,0) model is nonetheless a restrictive assumption, a single autoregressive lag on the first difference. Our order-selection results show that assumption was wrong for this data 75--83\% of the time; $(0,1,1)$, a moving-average model on the differenced series, fit better almost everywhere. A trend feature built from a poorly-fit model is not neutral, it is actively misleading, noise correlated with the raw signal it summarizes, and Section V-C's fixed-vs-selected comparison suggests that noise was suppressing the hybrid model's performance on FD002 and FD004. Once well-fit, on FD002 at least, it appears to supply real information the plain LSTM was not otherwise recovering, consistent with the window-length ablation (Section V-D), where the hybrid model's advantage is concentrated in a middle range of window lengths. We ran three further checks aimed directly at this, and report what they actually showed rather than the cleaner story we were hoping for.

\textbf{Is the order choice itself part of the story?} No. The AIC-preferred order's frequency was exactly stable across all six seeds within each dataset, $0.0$ percentage-point std on every order (full breakdown in our repository). The ARIMA step is deterministic given the data; whatever varies between seeds comes from the LSTM's own training stochasticity, not order instability.

\textbf{Is FD002's win concentrated in one operating regime, or broad?} Broad, with one exception. Stratified by FD002's six operating regimes, the order-selected hybrid beats the plain LSTM in five of six (0.3--1.4 cycles lower MAE) and loses in one (regime 5, by 1.1 cycles), an exception we cannot explain.

\textbf{Does FD002 have more ARIMA-explainable sensor trends, giving a direct mechanistic reason?} Weakly, not fully satisfyingly. Fitting ARIMA$(0,1,1)$ to 25 training units per dataset and measuring the fraction of each differenced sensor's variance the fitted trend explains ($R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{Var}(\text{residual})/\mathrm{Var}(\text{diff})$), FD002's mean $R^2$ (0.454) is statistically higher than all three others under a Welch's $t$-test (FD001: $+0.021$, $p<0.001$; FD003: $+0.041$, $p<0.001$; FD004: $+0.007$, $p=0.027$), but the gap against FD004, which matters most since it does not share FD002's win, is tiny despite clearing $p<0.05$. Significant but practically negligible is not an adequate mechanistic account.

\textbf{If naive concatenation is the problem, does a learned gate fix it?} No, not at the seed power that matters. The direct architectural fix for a poorly-fit trend feature misleading a fixed concatenation is to let the network learn how much to trust it. We built a gated-fusion variant, a per-timestep, per-channel sigmoid gate on the AIC-selected trend feature, trained under the same six-seed protocol on all four datasets (Table~\ref{tab:gated}). A single-seed pilot (seed 42) looked promising, competitive with the selected-order hybrid on three of four datasets. At full six-seed power that promise did not hold: gated fusion beats the selected-order hybrid's RMSE only on FD003, and a paired Wilcoxon test finds no significant difference from either the plain LSTM or the selected-order hybrid on any dataset (all $p>0.13$). This is the second time a single-seed result looked like a broad win and six seeds showed otherwise (Section V-A); a null result for the fix our own Discussion proposed is worth stating plainly.

Taken together, these four checks rule out order-choice instability, confirm the FD002 effect is broad rather than regime-concentrated, leave the most direct mechanistic hypothesis we tested, differential trend linearity, only weakly supported, and show that the one architectural fix our own diagnosis suggested does not produce a broad win either. We think this is a more honest place to leave the question than forcing a mechanism onto data that does not clearly support one.

\textbf{FD003's variance is worth a separate note.} The plain LSTM's across-seed std on FD003 (2.06 RMSE) is the largest of any model-dataset pair in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}. We have no confident explanation and would rather flag it as unexplained than invent one.

\textbf{What we would tell someone deciding whether to add this kind of feature.} Do not treat a single run, or even three seeds, as sufficient evidence either way; our own answer changed twice before six-seed power revealed that a naively fixed ARIMA order actively hurts this architecture and that fixing the order, not abandoning the idea, is where the real effect was hiding. Concretely, three checks are cheap enough to be routine: rerun under five or six seeds, not one to three (Section V-A); correct for multiple comparisons whenever the test repeats across datasets (Section V-B); and treat a fixed ARIMA order as a choice to be checked, not assumed (Section V-C).

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\textbf{ARIMA parameter estimation uses the full unit series, not an expanding window.} The trend feature's parameters ($\phi,\sigma^2$, Section III-C) are fit once per unit from that unit's entire available series: complete run-to-failure for a training unit, truncated history up to the prediction cycle for a test unit, since C-MAPSS test files hold no post-truncation readings. This does not leak future information into the reported test metrics, each engine's RUL is still predicted from data available up to its own truncation cycle, so Table~\ref{tab:main_results}'s comparisons remain valid. It does create a train/test asymmetry: an early- or mid-life training window is paired with a trend feature fit using that engine's complete future life, information a genuinely early-truncated engine would not yet have. We cannot rule out that this asymmetry, not the trend feature's causal content, contributes to the hybrid model's training-time behavior. A causal refit using only data through cycle $t$ to estimate cycle $t$'s parameters would remove it; we did not implement this variant.

The window-length ablation (Section V-D) uses six seeds on one dataset (FD001) and a shorter training budget than the main experiments; it is a single-dataset finding we have not attempted to replicate on FD002--FD004, or under the AIC-selected order. We tried four ARIMA orders, one recurrent architecture, and two fusion mechanisms (concatenation and gating, Section VI), but not an auxiliary trend-prediction loss or a sweep of LSTM hidden size or depth, any of which could plausibly interact with Section V-C's effect. We do not have an explanation for FD003's unusually high plain-model variance across seeds, and we are reporting it as an open observation rather than resolving it. We investigated why order selection produces a significant, seed-robust win specifically on FD002 (Section VI) and came away with only a weak, statistically-significant-but-practically-tiny signal on one candidate mechanism (differential trend linearity vs.\ FD004) and no explanation at all for why regime 5 within FD002 is the one place the win does not hold; a fuller account would likely need per-sensor rather than per-dataset analysis, which we did not attempt. Finally, every experiment here uses NASA's simulated turbofan data, and whether any of these patterns extend to real industrial IoT sensor streams with different noise and missingness characteristics is a question we are not in a position to answer.

\section{Conclusion}
We set out to test whether giving an LSTM an explicit per-sensor trend feature, computed with an ARIMA model, improves remaining useful life prediction on the NASA C-MAPSS benchmark. Under the fixed ARIMA order common in prior work, averaged over six seeds across all four sub-datasets, it does not: the hybrid model's mean RMSE is at or above a plain LSTM's everywhere, significantly so on FD004. Under a per-channel, AIC-selected order, the picture changes: the selected-order hybrid significantly and consistently beats the fixed-order hybrid on two of four datasets, and on FD002 it significantly and consistently beats the plain LSTM baseline itself, the one result we consider a genuine, seed-robust architectural win. A CNN-LSTM baseline with no ARIMA feature shows the same dataset-dependent pattern rather than a uniform result, significantly worse on FD002 and non-significantly best-in-table on FD003, evidence that FD002's resistance to added capacity and its response to order selection are both real. Getting to the order-selection answer took more checking than planned, an early, thinly-seeded version looked unstable and nearly convinced us to report it as noise, and only full six-seed power revealed it was not. The practical lesson generalizes past our own experiment: on a benchmark with 100--260 test engines, a single training run, or a small handful of them, is not enough to support a confident claim about whether an architectural change like this one helps, and we would encourage caution toward papers in this space, including the two we cite favorably in Section II, that do not report how many seeds their headline numbers rest on. A gated-fusion variant, our direct attempt at fixing naive concatenation with a learned gate, did not produce a broad win either at the same six-seed power (Section VI). Future work includes a mechanistic account of why FD002 specifically benefits, an auxiliary trend-prediction loss as an untested alternative to gating, a causal (expanding-window) ARIMA refit to remove the train/test asymmetry noted in Section VII, and validating the approach on proprietary industrial IoT sensor data.
